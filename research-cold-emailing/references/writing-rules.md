# Email drafting guide

Read this reference in full before drafting, rewriting, or reviewing an outreach email.

## The email's job

A first-contact research email should let a professor answer four questions quickly:

1. Who is this student?
2. Why did this student contact me specifically?
3. Is there evidence that the student could learn or contribute?
4. What small response is the student asking for?

The email does not need to prove that the student is already a researcher. It should demonstrate honest curiosity, preparation, and respect for the professor's time.

## Inputs required before writing

Do not mark an email `content-ready` until the writing inputs are available, and do not mark it `mail-ready` until the mail checks in [quality-review.md](quality-review.md) pass:

- the professor's verified name, role, institution, and official email source;
- one research source and an exact note of what was reviewed;
- one source-supported research detail;
- the independently assessed `Research fit` and `Current opportunity evidence`;
- one approved student introduction;
- one verified preparation example or specific learning goal;
- the intended opportunity or request;
- the approved signature and attachment rule.

When an input is missing, either ask for it or produce a clearly labeled provisional draft that identifies the unsupported section. Do not silently fill the gap or call a local text draft `mail-ready`.

## Choose the research angle

Select one angle that the student can explain in their own words.

### Finding-driven

Use when a result is surprising or consequential.

```text
The paper finds [specific result]. What caught my attention was [why the result changes an intuition, decision, or expected relationship].
```

### Method-driven

Use when the student is genuinely interested in how the evidence was produced.

```text
I was interested in how the study uses [dataset, experiment, model, archive, or identification strategy] to separate [one explanation] from [another explanation].
```

### Question-driven

Use when a source raises a clear follow-up question.

```text
The result made me wonder whether [specific extension or mechanism], especially when [relevant condition].
```

### Application-driven

Use when the research changes how people, firms, institutions, or policymakers make decisions.

```text
I found the connection between [research result] and [real decision] especially interesting because [plain-language consequence].
```

### Current-project-driven

Use only when a current official source explicitly describes ongoing work.

```text
Your lab's current project on [verified scope] caught my attention because [student's concrete reason].
```

Do not combine several angles merely to sound knowledgeable. One well-developed connection is stronger than a list.

## Match the wording to the evidence

- **Full paper reviewed:** discuss a result, method, design choice, limitation, or follow-up question accurately.
- **Abstract reviewed:** attribute the finding to the abstract and do not imply knowledge of the full analysis.
- **Project or lab page reviewed:** describe only the stated project scope.
- **Faculty profile reviewed:** refer to the listed research area, not a specific finding or current project.
- **Secondary source reviewed:** use it to locate a primary source whenever reasonably possible. Do not present a secondary summary as direct knowledge of the research.

Use restrained verbs such as `I read the abstract`, `I noticed`, `I was interested in`, or `The result made me wonder`. Avoid `I studied your work extensively` unless that is true and supported.

## Write the email by function

The sections below describe required functions, not a mandatory paragraph count. Combine adjacent sections when that produces a shorter, more natural email, but preserve identity, research connection, preparation, and a small ask.

### Subject

Use a plain subject that reveals the purpose. Good defaults include:

- `Undergraduate Research Inquiry`
- `Research Assistant Inquiry — [specific area]`
- `Question About Research Opportunities in [area]`

Use `undergraduate`, `master's`, `doctoral`, or another status only when it matches the profile. Avoid vague subjects such as `Request`, artificial urgency, flattery, or clickbait.

### Salutation

Use the title supported by the official source. `Dear Professor [Last Name],` is appropriate when the person is identified as a professor. Do not guess honorifics, credentials, name order, or preferred form of address.

### Paragraph 1: identity and purpose

State who the student is and why they are writing in one or two sentences.

Include only the identity details that help the professor understand the request:

- name;
- current institution and program status;
- field of study;
- direct purpose.

Example shape:

```text
I'm [name], a [year or program status] at [institution] studying [field], and I'm writing to ask about opportunities to support your research.
```

Match the purpose sentence to the opportunity evidence:

- **Confirmed opening:** `I'm writing to apply for the [verified role] described on [official source].`
- **Current invitation:** `I saw that your group currently invites students interested in [verified scope] to get in touch.`
- **None found or not checked:** `I'm writing to ask whether there might be opportunities to support your research.`
- **Stale or conflicting:** do not rely on the old or conflicting opportunity claim. Use exploratory wording and flag the uncertainty.

Without current opportunity evidence, avoid wording that presupposes a known opening, such as `I'm reaching out about opportunities in your lab` or `I would like to join your current project`.

Do not lead with weekly availability, a life story, graduate-school plans, or a list of qualifications.

### Paragraph 2: research connection

This is the most personalized part of the email. Write two or three sentences:

1. identify the exact paper, project, result, or method;
2. explain what genuinely caught the student's attention;
3. optionally state one focused question or implication.

The reaction must add something beyond paraphrasing the abstract. Useful reactions include:

- why the result challenged an intuition;
- which real decision it affects;
- why two mechanisms are difficult to separate;
- which extension the student became curious about;
- why the method is useful for answering the question.

Weak:

```text
Your research on behavioral finance closely aligns with my interests.
```

Stronger:

```text
The finding that additional information improved predicted repayment outcomes for far more borrowers than it improved predicted loan pricing caught my attention. It made me wonder whether that gap mainly reflects lender pricing rules or information the risk model still cannot observe.
```

The stronger version is useful only when that finding and interpretation are supported by the reviewed source.

### Paragraph 3: preparation and possible contribution

Choose one preparation example. Connect it to a plausible research activity without promising a role or inventing the professor's needs.

Good preparation examples include:

- cleaning or joining data;
- writing reproducible code;
- reading and summarizing literature;
- running statistical analysis learned in a course;
- documenting a dataset or workflow;
- applying a method in a verified project;
- domain knowledge relevant to the research question.

Use this reasoning sequence:

```text
verified experience → concrete action or method → relevance to learning or research support
```

Weak:

```text
I have strong analytical and communication skills and believe I would be a valuable addition to your team.
```

Stronger:

```text
In an applied statistics project, I used Python to clean a public rental dataset, document missing values, and estimate regression models. That experience gave me a starting point for careful data work, and I'd be interested in developing those skills in a research setting.
```

Do not turn familiarity into proficiency, coursework into prior research, participation into leadership, or a personal project into a deployed product.

### Paragraph 4: small ask

Ask for one low-friction next step.

Preferred shape:

```text
Would you be open to a brief conversation about your current work and whether student help could be useful?
```

Other appropriate asks depend on the user's goal:

- whether the professor anticipates research opportunities;
- whether another member of the group would be appropriate to contact;
- whether the professor could suggest material to read before a conversation.

Do not ask for several favors in the same first email. Do not assume an opening exists.

An exploratory question is allowed when opportunity evidence is `none found`. The email is asking the professor to reveal information that is not public; it is not asserting that an opportunity exists.

### Attachment sentence

Mention an attachment only after the correct file is visible in the reopened saved draft. One sentence is enough:

```text
I attached my résumé for context.
```

If attachment state cannot be verified, omit the sentence and report the missing check.

### Closing and signature

Thank the professor briefly and use the exact approved signature. Do not add a second pitch in the closing.

## Assemble and edit

After drafting the paragraphs:

1. remove repeated identity or research information;
2. keep one research idea and one student proof point;
3. replace abstract nouns with direct verbs;
4. split sentences carrying more than one main claim;
5. remove any sentence that could be sent unchanged to many professors;
6. verify every research and student claim against its source;
7. read the email aloud and replace language the student would not naturally say.

Aim for roughly 120–180 words before the signature when the evidence supports it. This is a practical default, not a reason to pad or abruptly cut a clear email.

## Tone

Use natural, direct language and varied sentence lengths. Contractions are acceptable when they match the student's voice. Sound curious and prepared, not certain of a match.

Avoid:

- stock openings such as `I hope this email finds you well` or `I am writing to express my interest`;
- generic praise, prestige language, or declarations of a perfect fit;
- a list of the professor's research keywords;
- a research paragraph that merely restates an abstract;
- several unrelated résumé achievements;
- unsupported enthusiasm such as `I am deeply passionate about...` or `I was fascinated by...` when the student's actual reaction is more tentative;
- mass-email language such as `Your research aligns perfectly with my interests`;
- excessive formality such as `I humbly request the privilege of...`;
- asking the professor to design a role before showing why the contact is relevant.

Do not manufacture certainty to make the email sound enthusiastic. A specific, tentative reaction such as `I found the result surprising because...` is more credible than a broad claim of passion. Preserve uncertainty when the student is still exploring the field.

## First contact and follow-up are different

For first contact, establish identity, specific relevance, preparation, and a small ask.

For a follow-up:

- reply in the existing thread;
- keep it materially shorter than the original message;
- refer to the prior email without guilt, pressure, or an apology for following up;
- repeat the core request in one sentence;
- add new information only when it is useful;
- do not resend the entire original email.

Example shape:

```text
Dear Professor [Last Name],

I'm following up on my note below about possible opportunities to support your research on [area]. I remain interested and would be glad to speak briefly if it would be useful. I understand if there is no availability at the moment.

Best,
[approved signature]
```

## Final drafting check

Before passing the draft to the quality review, confirm:

- the first sentence identifies the student and purpose;
- the research paragraph contains a traceable detail and genuine reaction;
- the preparation paragraph uses exactly one verified proof point;
- the request is small and easy to answer;
- the email does not imply an opening or current project without evidence;
- the purpose and ask match the recorded `Current opportunity evidence`;
- the wording reflects what the student actually reviewed;
- the body does not mention an attachment unless its presence can be verified.
